What Your Kitchen Told You This Thanksgiving, and How to Turn It Into a Plan

By DFC Home Improvement · November 2026 · Planning

Nobody learns more about their kitchen than the person who cooked Thanksgiving dinner in it. For one afternoon the room runs at full load: three dishes going at once, two people reaching into the same fridge, a sink full of pots before the turkey is even out, and a crowd that was supposed to stay in the living room standing exactly where you need to be. Whatever bothered you on Thursday is not a one-day problem. It is the same kitchen you use every night, just with the volume turned up.

That makes this week the best time of the year to think about the kitchen. The frustrations are specific and fresh, and specific is what a good design needs. Here are the seven things homeowners tell us they noticed over the holiday, what each one usually points to, and how to turn the list into a plan before it fades.

1. "I never had enough counter space where I was actually cooking"

Most kitchens have enough total counter. The problem is where it sits. If the landing space next to the range is a foot wide, or the only open stretch is across the room from the oven, every dish becomes a walk. Designers call the zone between the sink and the range the prep zone, and it is where the majority of the work happens. When that run is short, no amount of counter by the back door makes up for it.

What it points to: a layout change rather than new cabinets in the same place. Moving the sink or the range a few feet, extending a run, or adding an island or a peninsula puts the work surface where the work is. Note where you set the hot pans down and where you ran out of room. That is the measurement that matters.

2. "Everyone ended up in the kitchen anyway"

They always do. If the room was never meant to hold people and cooks at the same time, the guests stand in the walkway, the cook squeezes past them, and the dishwasher door becomes a hazard. The fix is not a bigger room so much as a room with a place for people to be: a peninsula or an island with seating on the far side, a wall opened to the dining or family room so the crowd can lean in without stepping in, or simply an aisle wide enough for two people to pass.

Opening a wall is the question we get asked most after the holidays. Many walls between a kitchen and a dining room can be opened, but some carry the floor above, and the answer depends on what is in the wall and above it, not on how it looks. That is a design question with a structural answer, and it is worth getting that answer early because it shapes everything else.

3. "The oven was the bottleneck"

One oven and a holiday menu do not get along. If you were juggling the bird, the sides and the rolls through a single door, the appliance plan is telling you something. The options range from a double wall oven, to a range plus a separate wall oven, to a warming drawer or a second small oven that takes the pressure off. Each needs its own circuit and its own cabinet, which is why appliance decisions come first in a design, not last.

The refrigerator belongs in the same conversation. If two people could not stand at the fridge at once, or the door swung into the walkway, note it. A counter-depth model, a door that opens the other way, or a second drawer refrigerator for drinks near the seating area keeps the guests out of the cooking zone.

4. "I could not find anything, or there was nowhere to put it"

Holiday cooking brings out the platters, the roasting pan and the appliances that live in the garage the rest of the year. If finding them meant emptying a cabinet onto the floor, storage is the issue, and it is usually a layout issue in disguise. Deep lower cabinets with doors are where things go to disappear; wide drawers put everything in view. A corner cabinet that only reaches halfway back wastes some of the most valuable space in the room. A pantry, whether a walk-in or a tall cabinet with roll-outs, takes the overflow off the counters year round.

Take a photo of what came out of the garage and the basement this week. That inventory tells a designer how much storage the kitchen actually needs, which is a better number than any rule of thumb.

5. "The room got hot and smelled like dinner for two days"

A kitchen that runs for six hours shows you exactly what the range hood does and does not do. Many hoods, including most over-the-range microwaves, recirculate the air through a small filter and send it right back into the room. The grease, the heat and the smell stay in the house. A hood that vents outdoors, sized to the range under it and ducted with as few turns as possible, is one of the biggest comfort upgrades a kitchen can get, and it is far easier to plan during a remodel than to add afterward, since the duct has to find its way through cabinets, ceilings or an exterior wall.

The same goes for windows and a door to the deck or patio. If opening a window was the only way to cool the room down, the ventilation plan, not the thermostat, is the thing to fix.

6. "I was cooking in my own shadow"

By four in the afternoon in late November the window is not helping, and a single ceiling fixture puts the light behind you when you face the counter. Lighting is the part of a kitchen most people only notice when it is wrong. A good plan layers it: recessed or ceiling lights for the room, under-cabinet lights for the counters, and pendants over an island or a peninsula where people sit. If you found yourself leaning to one side to see what you were chopping, write it down.

7. "The cleanup took longer than the meal"

The sink and the dishwasher are a zone of their own, and on a holiday that zone works harder than the range. A single-bowl sink that fits a roasting pan, a dishwasher close enough that plates go straight from the sink without dripping across the floor, a pull-out trash and recycling cabinet next to the sink, and a landing counter for dirty dishes that does not block the prep area all add up. If people were stacking plates on the stove because there was nowhere else, the cleanup zone is too small or in the wrong place.

Turn the list into a plan this week

The value of these observations fades fast. By mid-December the kitchen is back to weeknight duty and the frustrations feel smaller than they were. So before the leftovers are gone:

  1. Write the list down now. Not "the kitchen is too small," but the specific moments: where you ran out of counter, which door hit which person, what you could not find. Specific complaints become specific design decisions.
  2. Take photos of the room working. If you did not during the dinner, take them now with the counters as they normally are. Include the ceiling, the window wall and any wall you would like to open.
  3. Separate what you would keep from what you would change. Good appliances, a window you love, a floor that is in good shape. Keeping what works keeps the project focused.
  4. Decide what the kitchen has to do. Seat four for breakfast? Hold twenty for a holiday? Give two people room to cook at once? The answers drive the layout more than any finish choice.
  5. Think about the calendar. A kitchen designed in December and January, with selections made and permits filed in the winter, is built in the spring and finished long before next Thanksgiving.
